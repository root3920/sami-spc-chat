# Secure Parent Collective — Core Concepts and Frameworks

Plain-language summary of the frameworks taught in SPC (by Marcela Collier and the HIC Parenting coaches). Use these exact names and definitions. When a member wants more depth, point to the lesson listed in "Where it's taught" and search the transcripts.

## The goal of SPC
Help parents become "imperfect, secure parents": move from yelling, reactivity, punishment and power struggles to regulation, connection and confident, kind leadership. Growth is a practice, not an overnight change. SPC talks about developing the head, the heart and the hands of a secure parent. Closing line Marcela often uses: "It only takes understanding to transform your parenting."
Where it's taught: Welcome To SPC; U.S.E. Process (overview).

## The Secure Parent Journey (3 milestones)
- AWARE: "I stopped surviving."
- GROWING: "I started changing."
- LEADER: "I now help others grow."
The members-area modules use the same names: Aware Parent (U), Growing Parent (E), Leader Parent (S).
Where it's taught: "Your Secure Parent Journey" page in the Welcome module.

## The U.S.E. Process (core methodology)
- U — Unveil the need: understand the need underneath the behavior. Behavior is communication.
- S — Settle the nervous system: regulate yourself and help your child settle before any correction or teaching.
- E — Educate the behavior: teach the skill or the pro-social way to meet the need (limits, boundaries, consequences, the break-down method).
Where it's taught: Aware, Growing and Leader Parent modules (transcripts file T02).

## U: The 5 basic needs
Survival (including sensory needs), Power, Belonging, Freedom, Fun. Each has its own lesson in the Aware Parent module. Meeting a need today does not mean the child won't have the same need tomorrow (the cycle of needs).

## U: Social needs and the "Iceberg Effect"
In the series "Understanding Your Child's Needs", members draw an iceberg. Above the water: observable behaviors (hitting, screaming, tantrums, sibling fights, backtalk). Below the water: the basic needs and the social needs (what we need from another person). Social needs discussed in the series include approval, acceptance, intelligence (feeling capable), strength, sympathy and significance. Weeks 4–5 add the parent's blind spots: why we still react even when we "know better".

## S: The 4 behavior levels (based on Crisis Prevention Intervention, CPI)
1. Trigger
2. Anxiety
3. Defensiveness
4. Acting out (tantrums and meltdowns)
Catch the early levels early. During a meltdown the first goal is not to make the child stop; it is safety.
Where it's taught: Leader Parent module and "Calming Down Big Emotions" Parts 1–4.

## The 4 types of safety
Physical (is everyone physically safe?), emotional (does the child feel safe to express emotions to me?), psychological, and relational (are we okay in the relationship?).
Where it's taught: S: Behavior level 4 (meltdowns); Week 6 of "Understanding Your Child's Needs".

## The P.E.A.C.E. process (to manage triggers, yours and your child's)
- P — Presence
- E — Empathy (toward your own feelings and body sensations; it's okay to feel angry or triggered)
- A — Acknowledge the need
- C — Conscious choice: decide how you will respond
- E — Explore solutions to meet the need (troubleshoot together)
Triggers can be direct, indirect or trauma triggers. "If we don't own our triggers, our triggers own us."
Where it's taught: S: Behavior level 1: Trigger (workshop); Calming Down Big Emotions Part 2.

## The daily S.A.F.E. practice (the secure parent practice)
- S — Slow down
- A — Acknowledge what is happening in your body: your feelings and your needs
- F — Find the need
- E — Engage: decide how you will connect and respond
Members are encouraged to print the S.A.F.E. graphic or save it as their phone lock screen ("Your Daily S.A.F.E Practice" in the Welcome module). It is a practice, not a measurement of the parent.

## Secure attachment
- The 3 pillars of secure attachment: Safe, Seen, Soothed.
- About 70% consistency (not perfection) is enough to build security.
- Insecure attachment patterns covered: anxious (the "relationship watcher" who worries about getting it right), avoidant, and disorganized (when the child sometimes feels like "the enemy").
Where it's taught: "How to Raise a Secure Child" mini-series; "Growing as a Secure Parent With Secure Attachment" (Weeks 1–6).

## The 5 childhood core wounds (inner child)
Abandonment, Humiliation, Rejection, Injustice, Betrayal. Not every parent relates to every wound. The series helps parents notice how an old wound shows up in today's reactions and choose a different response ("Even if you yell again tonight, you are a secure parent").
Where it's taught: Mini Series "Understanding Your Inner Child to Transform Your Parenting".

## Discipline vs punishment
Discipline is not eliminating behavior; behavior is communication. Discipline is finding the feelings, needs and wants underneath the behavior and developing the skills the child doesn't have yet, so they can communicate those needs in a pro-social way that doesn't hurt themselves or others. The goal of discipline is to train children to manage their emotions, not just to get a moment of quiet.
The skills beneath the behavior include: emotional regulation, frustration tolerance, flexibility and impulse control. Other ideas in the series: preventative discipline (secure parents don't wait for the behavior to happen) and corrective teaching when discipline gets hard. Skills take time: a skill taught today is not mastered tomorrow.
Where it's taught: Mini Series "Guiding Behavior Without Reactivity" (Weeks 1–6).

## Co-parenting dynamics
Three insecure dynamics and the goal: the policing dynamic, the uneven load dynamic, the domino effect dynamic, and the secure co-parenting dynamic everyone works toward. Parenting feels different for each adult; nobody wakes up wanting to undermine their partner.
Where it's taught: Season 3, "Becoming a Secure Co-Parenting Team".

## Parent stress responses
Fight, flight or freeze. Knowing your own stress response helps you notice it and come back to presence. Coaching often moves a parent from a limiting belief ("everything is my fault", "I'm not enough") to a more expansive belief ("this is hard, and I am capable"), then softens the body where the stress lives, then responds to the child without the agenda of "fixing" or "putting out a fire".
Where it's taught: Library of Coaching Highlights (for example the bedtime sessions).
